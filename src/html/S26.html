My Goals

For this work term, I had 3 goals I wanted to accomplish.

  • Improve my ability to analyze and determine problems quickly
  • Improve my ability to multitask on many projects
  • Learn Active Directory/Microsoft 365
I chose these goals as an extension to my last term. Since I had virtually no adjustment period, learning new things and being more productive was a big focus. This got me more involved in more aspects of the company, and allowed me to handle greater responsibility on the job.

Improve my ability to analyze and determine problems quickly

The problem solving process

This goal was important for me because this was the first time that I was in an environment where I knew how things worked, I knew our infrastructure, I knew our software, and I knew the services we use. I wanted to see how well I can debug issues, or resolve technical issues without assistance from my supervisor. Luckily for me, I had the perfect (and most terrifying) opportunity to work on this skill.

At Connect Tech, there were 3 people in the IT space, my supervisor Mark, the IT Director, Matt Ferraro and me. Mark took a vacation for 2 weeks during late July into early August. During this time, Matt was also off due to personal reasons. I was running the IT department virtually solo for 2 weeks. In one way this was great, because it meant that my supervisors had complete trust in me to manage things in their absence, but in another way, if things went really bad, there was only so much I could do to resolve certain issues.

This gave me a great chance to work on this goal, since if something major went wrong, I had to act fast to resolve the issue. Imagine if the internet went down company wide, for a tech company! That would be crazy! Well it did. One Thursday morning I got reports that the internet was down. The first thing I did was instantly call the Internet Service Provider (ISP). I had to determine whether this was an infrastructure failure on our end, or the provider's. I learned from that phone call that we had somehow reached our bandwidth limit, meaning many people were not getting any internet. This meant that it was somebody internal causing the issue. But before I could find who was causing the issue, the internet came back up. I didn't find the source of the issue, but this diagnosis took me 20 minutes, and most of this time came from the phone call with the ISP.

This scenario was proof that I was improving this skill. I maintained composure and started searching for a solution instantly and narrowed down the root cause quickly. I was proud of what I came up with, even if I never got to find who the culprit was. Later, my supervisor and I discovered it was one of our AI team members downloading a large language model. The rest of my time solo went smoothly, and all issues were resolved quickly. This was a skill I improved on greatly.

Improve My Ability to Multitask On Multiple Projects

Multitasking is not ideal, but it is inevitable. There will always be many projects, and many issues on the go at once, so I wanted to improve on this skill. Luckily, the IT department at Connect Tech is very involved in every aspect of the company (whether actully IT related or not) so I got to practice this a lot.

The first step I found to successfully multitask is to keep all of your ongoing tasks in some type of dashboard, where you can monitor their status, take notes on them, and see what you have pending, and upcoming. Luckily for me, the IT department's previous Co-op was awesome and built a help desk tool that does exactly this (I was the previous Co-op). Since I could track everything at once, I was in a good state to start knocking off tasks quickly.

With a bit of experimentation I found my best method for multitasking. I would juggle a couple tasks, in things completely separate from each other. I would usually do one programming task, one setup task, and one help desk task at a time. These tasks are generally unrelated and I could work on one until I had no option but to wait for something to complete, and then switch task, and repeat until the tasks are complete. I found that this worked the best for me as I was able to get more work done, without being overwhelmed. This helped me ensure that I missed no tasks and got as much as I could done in a day.

Learn Active Directory/Microsoft 365

Active Directory Logo

The Microsoft suite of tools for user management is one of the most widely used tools, and can be found in nearly all companies in some way, so I wanted to learn this suite of tools. This was especially important as many issues that come up on day to day basis are related to out user management system (Active Directory) or email related issues (Microsoft 365). This was also a crucial skill to improve on for my time with no supervisors.

I spoke with my supervisor about taking on more resposibility on this regard, and he was comfortable with allowing me to start taking resposibility for certain aspects of these tools. My resposibilities began with simple user management, such as creating users, updating passwords and other simple tasks. This was easy to learn so my resposibilities quickly grew. Before I knew it, I was learning about our DHCP and DNS servers, Security groups and host configurations, and our system backup setups. I learned about all of these tools, and it enabled me to do routine maintenance for our backup server, which led to our total storage use being reduced, less errors occuring and a cleaner catalog of backups. It also enabled me to resolve issues faster on the user side, being able to quickly check DHCP Leases, or host name conflicts to resolve networking issues. This suite of tools let me make a big difference and resolve issues much faster than I once was able to.

I was also given more responsibility with Microsoft 365, I was taught how to manage mailing lists and user accounts, resolve sending issues and debug and email problems that arose. Overall I learned a lot about these tools, and they allowed me to resolve issues more efficiently with less supervision, and it set me up for success when I was the solo IT employee on-site. These skills I learned are timeless, and I'm sure I will be using them again in the future.

What I Did On The Job

This work term I did not take on a specific project. It was more general, I was making improvements to existing systems, creating new scripts to do maintenance tasks quicker, managing user setup, and doing research for new infrastructure that was under consideration. The biggest task I took on was a revamp of the internal company site DDCS. I suggested to my supervisor some changes that I could make that would make his life easier, improve consistency across the system, and improve the appearance of the site. I was given the green light for these things, so I updated the site with an email list manager, a full permission system, and dark mode. The email list manager allowed automated email recipients to be changed from the site directly, without needing to alter any code. The permission system was a new system to allow fine grained access to specific users to certain functionality across the site, and dark mode was just a different theme for the site to help reduce some of the eye strain that employees can suffer from with this type of work. For these tasks I used the theory that I learned in class to influence my design decisions, but the code was learned entirely by myself.

Apart from those more major changes, my day to day work usually consisted of small bug fixes on the sites, implementing change requests we received, setting up new employee systems, helping employees with technical issues, researching new ways to improve on our existing system, and developing a testing suite for our ERP system. I think the most interesting part of these tasks was just how involved it was across the entire company. I found myself in a meeting at one point with the CFO, Director of accounting, a Senior Hardware Engineer and an Assistant Controller at one point. For most Co-ops, this would never happen, and this was the best part of the job. I felt like my role expanded beyond that of a Co-op student, and I was viewed in a different light by people.

Some other notable things I did was research and set up the companies visitor management system. This included configuring new infrastructure to sync with our existing systems to maintain logs of who visits the company, and designing visitor badges for our visitors. The system I selected and setup received full approval from the President, who loved the new system. I also wrote many scripts to help with tasks that needed to be completed, but took far too much manual labor, such as a script to compare the company's SVN repository. We were in the process of switching servers and needed to ensure all the data was saved and transferred properly.

Another major aspect of my job is customer service. Being in the IT department I had to assist many employees with their day to day needs whenever issues arose with the tech they were using. This meant I got to meet people from all departments and build relationships with all people in the company. This was good for me as it allowed me to become more embedded in the company and get to know the company's business processes more.
